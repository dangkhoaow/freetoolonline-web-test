Frequently Asked Questions

How tall is the Matterhorn?

Its summit sits at 4,478 m (14,692 ft) above sea level, in the Pennine Alps on the border between Zermatt, Switzerland, and Breuil-Cervinia, Italy.

Why does the Matterhorn have four separate faces?

It is a classic glacial "horn" peak: glaciers eroded the original mountain mass from several sides at once, cutting back toward the center until only four steep faces and the sharp ridges between them remained.

What are the names of the four ridges?

The Hoernli ridge (northeast, the standard Swiss route), the Zmutt ridge (northwest), the Lion or Italian ridge (southwest), and the Furggen ridge (southeast).

When was the Matterhorn first climbed?

July 14, 1865, via the Hoernli ridge, led by Edward Whymper with guides Michel Croz and Peter Taugwalder (father and son). On the descent, four of the seven climbers fell to their deaths when the rope parted - a tragedy that made global headlines. The Lion ridge was first climbed three days later, on July 17, 1865, led by guide Jean-Antoine Carrel.

Why does snow not cover the whole mountain?

The Matterhorn's faces are so steep that much of the snow simply cannot stick - bare rock stays exposed on large sections of the mountain even in winter, unlike gentler peaks that stay blanketed in white.

Is the Matterhorn dangerous to climb?

Yes. More than 500 climbers are estimated to have died on the Matterhorn since 1865, a widely cited average of roughly a dozen a year - among the highest tolls of any single peak, despite the standard Hoernli-ridge route being a popular guided climb.

Is this a scientific terrain model?

No. It is an educational explorer using published figures - the ridge geometry, snow pattern, and marker position are schematic teaching cues, not survey terrain.
