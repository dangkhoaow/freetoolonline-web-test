White Sands 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-08-28

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer shows White Sands as a field of pale, asymmetric barchan dune ridges: a gentle windward rise on one side of each crest and a steeper leeward slip face on the other, tracing how the prevailing southwest wind pushes the gypsum sand downwind. Toggle moonlit night lighting to see the pale gypsum sand read cool and glow, and the soaptree yucca markers scattered across the dune slopes.

White Sands National Park protects part of the largest gypsum dune field on Earth, covering about 275 sq mi (712 sq km) in New Mexico's Tularosa Basin - roughly 40 percent of the field lies inside the park, with the rest inside White Sands Missile Range next door. The gypsum itself washed down from the surrounding Sacramento and San Andres Mountains into ancient Lake Otero, which dried up long ago; wind has since ground the leftover gypsum crystals into the fine white sand that builds the dunes today. Dunes here reach up to about 60 ft (18 m) tall and migrate up to about 30 ft (9 m) per year. Unlike typical quartz sand, the pale gypsum reflects sunlight rather than absorbing it, so the surface stays notably cooler underfoot than a typical hot desert dune even under strong sun. The area was first protected as White Sands National Monument in 1933, then redesignated White Sands National Park in December 2019. Soaptree yucca survives on the moving dunes by stretching its stem several meters upward to keep pace with the rising sand, and ranger-led full-moon hikes are a popular park program because the pale gypsum sand reflects moonlight so strongly at night.

The whole scene - the asymmetric dune ridges, the pale gypsum color signature, the moonlight toggle, and the yucca markers - is built and drawn on your own device with WebGL through the site-vendored three.js engine. Orbit the scene to trace how each ridge rises gently on one side and drops steeply on the other, and toggle the moonlit lighting to see the color shift cool. Nothing about your visit is uploaded to a server, and the engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached.

This is an educational approximation - the dune shapes, color gradient, moonlight toggle, and yucca markers are schematic teaching cues, not survey data.
