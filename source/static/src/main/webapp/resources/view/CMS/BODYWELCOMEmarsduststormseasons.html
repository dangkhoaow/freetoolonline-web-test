Mars Dust Storm Seasons 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-09-06

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer shows why Mars has a dust-storm season at all: its orbit is noticeably egg-shaped (eccentricity 0.0935, about 5.6 times more eccentric than Earth's), so the extra sunlight Mars receives near perihelion and the southern-hemisphere summer solstice drives the strongest atmospheric circulation of the Martian year.

Why a season exists, and how often it goes global

Roughly every three Mars years, a regional dust storm inside that window grows big enough to wrap the whole planet. Mars moves along an orbit ellipse drawn to its real eccentricity, and the tan-highlighted arc marks the recognized dust-storm season. Trigger a global storm inside that window and the scene thickens the dust shroud while the illustrative solar-panel-power bar drops to the low level that left NASA's Opportunity rover unable to recharge - the 2018 storm that ended its 15-year mission in February 2019.

What you can change in this scene

  • Day slider (0 to 687, one full Mars year) updates the areocentric solar longitude (Ls) readout live
  • Play year auto-scrubs the whole orbit; Jump to dust season lands inside the tan-highlighted season arc
  • Trigger a global dust storm is enabled only while the slider sits inside the dust-storm season, since global storms have never been observed outside it
  • Facts panel states the current Ls, day of year, distance from the Sun, whether the slider is inside the dust-storm season, the atmospheric-opacity (tau) reading, and an illustrative solar-panel-power bar; inside the season arc, small illustrative dust patches also appear on Mars
  • Orange perihelion, yellow southern-summer-solstice, and blue aphelion markers stay fixed on the orbit ellipse while you scrub
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom
  • Runs fully in the browser - renders on your device with WebGL, no account, no upload; the three.js engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached, and nothing about your visit is sent to a server

Who this scene is for

Students studying planetary science, and anyone curious why a single dust storm could end a working Mars rover, can use the day slider to connect the orbit shape to the season, and the season to the real 2018 event.

Where the scene simplifies

This is an educational approximation, not a climate simulation. Mars's position on the ellipse is a smooth Keplerian model using the real eccentricity and perihelion/aphelion distances, not a day-by-day ephemeris; the in-season tau curve is shaped to land within Curiosity's documented range rather than a measured record, and the solar-panel-power bar is an illustrative decreasing function of tau, not NASA's measured power curve for Opportunity's array. Only the 2018 and 2007 storm tau values, Curiosity's seasonal range, and the Ls 180-340 window are direct published figures.
