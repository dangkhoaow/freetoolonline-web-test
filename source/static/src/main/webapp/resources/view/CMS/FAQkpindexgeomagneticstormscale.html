Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Kp index?

A 3-hour planetary geomagnetic-activity index on an integer 0-9 scale, introduced by Julius Bartels in 1949. The official value is computed by GFZ Potsdam from 13 ground magnetometer stations at geomagnetic latitude 44-60 degrees; NOAA separately publishes a real-time estimate.

How does Kp map to NOAA's G-scale?

Kp=5 is G1 (Minor), Kp=6 is G2 (Moderate), Kp=7 is G3 (Strong), Kp=8 is G4 (Severe), and Kp=9 is G5 (Extreme). Kp 0-4 sits below the G-scale with no storm level assigned.

What happened during the May 2024 superstorm?

The "Gannon storm" reached Kp=9 (G5 Extreme), a minimum Dst of -412 nT and a maximum Ap of 271 - the strongest geomagnetic storm since the October 2003 Halloween storms - with aurora reported as far south as Alabama and Northern California.

Does higher Kp always mean aurora further south?

Generally yes: at low Kp, aurora is usually confined to the high-latitude auroral oval near the poles; around Kp 7 it can reach mid-latitudes; at Kp 8-9 it can become visible from low latitudes, as in May 2024.

Is the magnetometer trace on this page real data?

No - it is a synthetic, illustrative pattern whose swing widens with the picked Kp value, not a live or historical magnetometer feed.

How is this different from the Aurora, Carrington Event, and Earth Magnetosphere pages?

The Aurora page explains the light-emission mechanism itself. The Carrington Event page covers one specific 1859 historical storm. The Earth Magnetosphere page shows the field's static structure. This page is the numeric G1-G5 severity scale, compared across Kp values and the real May 2024 case.

Is the on-screen field and aurora geometry physically accurate?

No - the field arcs, aurora-ring size, and magnetometer trace are illustrative teaching cues, not a computed field model or a live space-weather feed. The Kp thresholds, G-scale mapping, and May 2024 case figures are the real NOAA/USGS-published values.
