How to Check a US Drug Recall - Step by Step

Last reviewed: 2026-09-12

The drug recall lookup searches the official US FDA drug enforcement database in three steps: open the page, type a medicine or brand, and read the report cards. No account is needed, and the newest recalls are already on screen when the page loads.


Step 1 - open the lookup

Go to the drug recall lookup. The page is useful the moment it loads: the 8 newest US drug recall enforcement reports render automatically, newest first, so you can see what a report card looks like before typing anything. The classification table below the results explains what Class I, Class II, and Class III mean.


Step 2 - search a medicine, brand, or maker

Type a keyword such as acetaminophen, a brand name, or a company into the box and press Search recalls or hit Enter. One search checks four fields at once - the product description, the recalling firm, and the openFDA brand and generic names - so the brand name and the active ingredient both find the same reports. The optional state dropdown narrows results to firms based in one US state - leave it on Any state unless you are researching a specific company. Up to 25 matching reports return, newest first.


Step 3 - read the report cards right

Each card shows the exact product description, a brand/generic line when openFDA enrichment is present, the recalling firm with its city and state, the classification badge, the current status, whether the recall was voluntary or FDA-mandated, the reason, the affected lot codes, the initiation date, and where the product was distributed. Class I matches - FDA's most serious category, meaning a reasonable probability of serious health consequences or death - are flagged with a red banner. Compare the lot number and NDC printed on your package or pharmacy label against the lot codes line: a recall usually covers specific lots, not every package.


Step 4 - when nothing matches

A no-match result states plainly that no enforcement report matches your query, suggests trying the generic name instead of the brand (or the brand instead of the generic), and links to fda.gov. That is not always all-clear: enforcement reports lag public announcements by days to weeks, medical device and animal-drug recalls live in separate FDA systems, and dietary supplements are FDA-regulated foods - use the food recall lookup for those. For a recall you just saw in the news, check fda.gov/safety/recalls directly. And for a prescription medicine, never stop on your own - ask your pharmacist or prescriber first. This lookup is informational, not medical advice.
