Frequently Asked Questions

Why does Kilauea look so different from a typical volcano cone?

Kilauea is a shield volcano - its lava is thin and flows easily, so it spreads into a low, very broad dome instead of piling up into a steep cone. Its summit sits at only about 1,247 m, far lower than a steep stratovolcano like Vesuvius, and its slopes are gentle enough that the overall shape reads more like a wide dome than a mountain peak.

Is the Halema'uma'u lava lake always active?

No. Halema'uma'u has hosted an active lava lake during several separate eruptive episodes since 2008, but the lake has also drained or gone quiet between episodes. This explorer shows the lava lake toggled on by default as the crater's best-known state, but the real lake's presence changes with Kilauea's eruptive activity.

What are the East Rift Zone and Southwest Rift Zone?

They are two long fracture zones that radiate outward from Kilauea's summit caldera, where magma can travel underground and erupt from fissures far from the summit itself. The East Rift Zone is the longer and more active of the two - it fed the Pu'u 'O'o vent from 1983 to 2018 and the destructive lower Puna eruption in 2018.

How big is Kilauea's summit caldera?

The summit caldera is about 4 km by 3.2 km across, formed by past collapses as magma drained from underneath. The smaller Halema'uma'u crater sits within that larger caldera and is where most of the recent lava-lake activity has been observed.

Is Kilauea part of Hawaii Volcanoes National Park?

Yes. Kilauea sits inside Hawaii Volcanoes National Park on Hawaii Island, on the southeast flank of the much larger Mauna Loa. The park was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, recognizing its exceptional record of volcanic activity and its distinctive Hawaiian ecosystems.
