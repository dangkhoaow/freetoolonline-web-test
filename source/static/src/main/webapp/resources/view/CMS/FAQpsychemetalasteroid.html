Frequently Asked Questions

When did Psyche launch and fly past Mars?

It launched from NASA's Kennedy Space Center on 2023-10-13 and flew past Mars for a gravity assist on 2026-05-15, at a closest approach of about 4,609 km (2,864 mi).

Why did Psyche need a Mars flyby?

The flyby's gravity assist added about 1,000 mph to the spacecraft's speed and bent its orbital plane about 1 degree relative to the Sun - without spending any propellant - to line it up for its 2029 arrival at asteroid 16 Psyche.

How big is asteroid 16 Psyche, and is it solid metal?

Psyche measures about 278 by 232 by 164 km (mean diameter about 222 km). Its bulk density, about 3.98 g/cm3, is well below solid iron-nickel metal (about 8 g/cm3), so scientists think it is a porous mix of metal and rock, not one solid metal ball.

When does Psyche arrive and how long does it study the asteroid?

Asteroid gravity is expected to capture the spacecraft in late July 2029, with science operations beginning in August 2029. The mission plan calls for at least 26 months of study across progressively different orbits, ending 2031-10-31.

What does Psyche run on for propulsion?

A solar-electric propulsion system: four SPT-140 Hall-effect thrusters drawing up to 4.5 kW from its solar arrays, carrying about 922 kg of xenon propellant.

Is the on-screen Psyche shape and orbit to scale?

No - Psyche's body and the spacecraft's cruise route and orbit rings are procedural shapes for teaching purposes, not a precise shape model or a physically simulated flight path or orbit. Only the dates and figures cited in the facts panel are real published figures.

How is this different from the Hayabusa2 or DART mission explorers?

Hayabusa2 landed twice and returned a physical sample from a carbon-rich asteroid; DART deliberately impacted an asteroid to test planetary defense. This page instead replays Psyche's cruise-flyby-orbit timeline at a metal-rich asteroid that the spacecraft studies from orbit, never landing or impacting.

Where do these figures come from?

NASA and NASA Science mission updates, the JPL Psyche press kit, and the peer-reviewed studies Vernazza et al. 2021, Ferrais et al. 2020, and Shepard et al. 2017/2021 (via JPL's Small-Body Database and Wikipedia's cited figures).
