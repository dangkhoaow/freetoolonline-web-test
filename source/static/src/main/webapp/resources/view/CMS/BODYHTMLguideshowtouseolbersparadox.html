How to Use the Olbers' Paradox 3D Explorer - Step by Step

Last reviewed: 2026-08-03

Open the Olbers' Paradox 3D Explorer, toggle the mode button, then scrub the shell slider to see why the night sky stays dark instead of glowing as bright as a star's surface.


Toggle the model button

Toggle the model button between "naive" (infinite, static, eternal universe) and "real" (finite age plus expansion). Naive mode lets the shell slider reach 12; real mode caps it at 6 of 12, standing for light that has not had time to reach us yet.


Scrub the shell slider

Scrub the shell slider to reveal or hide concentric shells of stars live. The label above the slider reports the current count out of the mode's maximum, and each shell you add stands for a greater distance from the center.


Watch the sky-glow backdrop

Watch the sky-glow backdrop sphere brighten as you add shells. In naive mode the glow can approach full brightness; in real mode it is capped far lower, illustrating why an infinite eternal starfield's sky would blaze while the real sky stays dark.


Note the red-tinted shells near the horizon

Note that in real mode, shells near the illustrative horizon are tinted from white toward red and dimmed, standing for cosmological redshift pushing their light out of the visible band before it reaches us.


Read the facts panel's history and figures

Read the facts panel for the current shell count, the illustrative sky-coverage percentage (explicitly disclosed as a pedagogical device, not a real photometric calculation), and the history behind each mode: Kepler's 1610 argument, Olbers' 1823 paradox, Poe's 1848 Eureka essay, and Kelvin's 1901 resolution in naive mode; the universe's age of about 13.8 billion years (Planck 2018) in real mode.


Compare the two models

Compare the two models directly: switch to naive mode with a high shell count to see the glow climb, then back to real mode to see it stay capped low - the same slider, two very different skies.
