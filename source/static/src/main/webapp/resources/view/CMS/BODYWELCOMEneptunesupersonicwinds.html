Neptune Supersonic Winds 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-07-25

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer scrubs through 3 real observation eras of Neptune's Great Dark Spot and compares Neptune's real peak measured winds against Saturn, Jupiter, and Earth, so you can see why Neptune has the fastest winds ever measured on a planet.

Neptune's equatorial diameter is about 49,528 km, and it completes one rotation in about 16.1 hours on average - with different latitudes actually spinning at different rates, about 18 hours at the equator and about 12 hours near the poles, because the planet is not a rigid body. Neptune orbits roughly 30 times farther from the Sun than Earth does, so it receives about 900 times less sunlight - yet peak measured winds near its Great Dark Spot reached roughly 2,000-2,400 km/h, the fastest winds ever recorded on any planet in the solar system. Why a planet with so little solar energy has the strongest winds is an open research question tied to Neptune's internal heat, fast rotation, and low atmospheric drag. In 1989, NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft discovered the Great Dark Spot, an anticyclonic storm roughly the size of Earth, centered around 20 degrees south latitude; consecutive Voyager 2 images captured full rotations of the storm system about 18 hours apart. Unlike Jupiter's Great Red Spot, which has been observed continuously since at least 1831, the Great Dark Spot was not permanent - Hubble Space Telescope observations in the mid-1990s found no trace of it. These figures come from NASA's Neptune Facts page, the NASA NSSDC Planetary Fact Sheet, NASA/JPL's Great Dark Spot image sequence, and NASA's reporting on the Hubble storm-lifecycle observations.

  • Era slider (3 stops) redraws Neptune with the Great Dark Spot present or absent at each real, documented observation era
  • Facts panel shows the era, what was observed, and a running table of all 3 eras with the active one highlighted
  • A wind-speed comparison table ranks Neptune's peak measured winds against Saturn, Jupiter, and Earth
  • A small glowing dot ring marks the storm's fixed latitude when present, and spins with Neptune's rotation
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom
  • Runs fully in the browser with the vendored three.js engine - no account, no upload

Everything renders on your device with WebGL. The 3D engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is cached.

This is an educational approximation - Neptune's cloud bands are drawn as artistic color stripes, not a photographic texture, and the storm's oval shape and on-screen size are stylized. Whether the storm appears in each era, and its approximate 20 degree south latitude, reflect the real published observations cited above.
