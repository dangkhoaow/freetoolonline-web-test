Iguazu Falls 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-08-29

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer shows Iguazu Falls in cross-section: a curved basalt cliff line where the Iguazu River's plateau drops away into about 275 separate cascades spread across a 2.7 km crescent. Near the center of that crescent the cliff cuts deeper into the plateau at the Devil's Throat (Garganta do Diablo), a U-shaped chasm about 150 m wide and 700 m long where the tallest drop, about 82 m, sends up a constant column of spray.

The falls sit on the border between Argentina's Iguazu National Park and Brazil's Iguacu National Park, a few kilometers upstream of where the Iguazu River joins the much larger Parana River. Average flow is about 1,746 cubic m per second, but that figure swings widely with the season - down to a few hundred cubic m per second in a dry spell, and past 12,000 during flood peaks, when many of the smaller cascades merge into a single unbroken wall of water. The falls formed on a basalt plateau laid down by ancient lava flows, since carved back by the river over millions of years. Both national parks are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, and the falls were named one of the New7Wonders of Nature in 2011.

  • Toggle the flow band to compare a dry-season trickle against flood-season spray across the cliff face
  • Toggle the Devil's Throat marker to locate the deepest, U-shaped chasm in the crescent
  • Read width, height, and flow-rate figures in the panel
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom
  • Runs fully in the browser with the vendored three.js engine - no account, no upload

For a canyon carved by a much larger river over a much longer drop, open the Grand Canyon 3D Explorer, or compare the world's deepest point of any kind in the Mariana Trench 3D Explorer.

The explorer first paints a schematic escarpment, then streams in the real measured terrain of this reach: elevation from Mapzen and AWS Open Data terrain tiles (derived from NASA and USGS DEMs) and true-color satellite imagery that contains modified Copernicus Sentinel-2 data, baked into a small bundled dataset and cached in your browser after the first visit. On that real mesh the relief is shown at true 1:1 scale relative to the mapped width, so the falls' 60-82 m drop reads as a subtle step next to the roughly 12 km view - the Sentinel-2 imagery, with the Iguazu River crossing the plateau at about 150 m above sea level and dropping into the gorge at about 90 m, carries the scene. The falling water, mist, and the schematic escarpment are teaching cues, not measured hydrology. Everything runs on your device with WebGL and nothing about the visit is sent to a server; if the terrain data or WebGL is unavailable the page keeps the schematic escarpment and the facts panel above still renders.

This is an educational approximation - the cliff profile, crescent shape, and vertical scale are schematic teaching cues, not survey data.
