Acrocanthosaurus 3D Viewer

Last reviewed: 2026-09-10

This page renders an Acrocanthosaurus as a 3D model you can spin in the browser. Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom, toggle a 1.8 m person beside it for scale, and click the head, an arm, the tail, or a leg to read a fact about that part. Everything runs on your device - no account, no upload.

How to steer the model

Four buttons beneath the scene each name your next action rather than leaving you to guess. The spin button reads Stop rotation while the model turns and Auto-rotate once you pause it. The motion button switches between Idle motion and Stand still: with Idle motion on, the animal stays put while its legs swing and its body bobs in a slow, steady rhythm - a stand-and-sway rather than a walk across the scene, so the pose stays in front of you and its stance reads clearly. The third button drops in the human figure to gauge how the animal would tower over you. The fourth expands the viewer to fullscreen for a closer look at the skull, arms, and tail: the model grows to fill the screen, the facts panel rides along as a translucent dark overlay, and the buttons stay within reach, so the note for the part you last clicked keeps reading and you can still stop or start the spin without leaving the larger view. Dragging and zooming both stay within sensible limits: the tilt stops short of flipping the animal over, and the scroll or pinch zoom holds between a close pass at the skull and a full-body framing, so the Acrocanthosaurus never slips off screen or turns upside down no matter how hard you spin or scroll. On a phone or a modest laptop the viewer notices the touch screen or the smaller processor and quietly eases off the graphics load, so the model keeps turning smoothly instead of stuttering. And because a spin only counts as a tap when your finger barely moves, dragging to rotate never accidentally pops open a body-part note - that side panel opens only when you actually mean to tap a part.

Reading the facts panel

Click a body part and the side panel swaps to that region's note; click an empty patch of the scene and the full fact sheet comes back. That lets you move between the skull, the arms, and the tail without losing the overview.

Each note also separates what the fossil records from what an artist inferred: the head note says the skull shape follows the bone while the lips and color are reconstruction, the arm note points out that the finger count is read from the preserved fossil hand, and the tail note explains how it counterbalanced the body over the hips and, in some theropods, doubled as a weapon - so a click tells you how confident each detail really is.

What Acrocanthosaurus was

Acrocanthosaurus atokensis was a large theropod from the Early Cretaceous of North America, roughly 125 to 100 million years ago, and was among the apex predators of its ecosystem in what is now the south-central United States. Its vertebrae bear unusually tall neural spines along the neck, back, and hips - researchers interpret those spines as anchoring a low muscular ridge along the dorsal line, not a tall sail like Spinosaurus. Some allow the spines a display role as well, but that stays interpretive, because fossils keep the bone and not the soft-tissue outline.

Who named it and where it was found

J. Willis Stovall and Wann Langston named the genus in 1950. Specimen NCSM 14345 ("Fran") comes from the Antlers Formation of Oklahoma and is among the most complete skeletons known for the genus; the animal is also recorded from the Twin Mountains Formation of Texas, not only Oklahoma, so its known range spanned both present-day states. Published length estimates center on about 11 to 11.5 m; mass estimates span about 4.4 to 8.4 tonnes across studies - this viewer uses about 6.2 t as a mid-range panel value within that range.

What runs on your device, and what the model is not

The Acrocanthosaurus viewer runs on your device with WebGL, without an account and without sending anything to a server. It downloads the 3D engine and model once, checks the model, and keeps them cached, so returning to the page brings the scene up quickly instead of fetching it again. The shape you see first is drawn in the browser from simple geometry the moment the engine is ready, so the frame is never left blank; if a verified, more detailed model of this species is available it quietly takes the place of that first drawing, and if none is published the first drawing stays. If your browser has WebGL turned off, the reference table above and the facts panel still work, so you can read the length, mass, and timeline even when the model cannot draw. The skin tone and pattern are an artistic reconstruction, because fossils do not preserve color or soft tissue; this model is not a fossil-accurate skeleton.
