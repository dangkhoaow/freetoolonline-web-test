Planet Atmosphere Scale-Height 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-08-02

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

Drag the altitude control on this browser explorer to compare how fast Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, and Titan's real atmospheres thin out with height - each world decays at its own published rate.

Every atmosphere in the solar system follows the same barometric relationship: pressure falls off roughly exponentially with altitude, and the height over which it drops by a factor of about 2.72 (Euler's number, e) is called the scale height. The formula is H = kT / (M g), where T is temperature, M is the average mass of one gas molecule, and g is surface gravity - so a hot, light-molecule, low-gravity atmosphere puffs up into a large scale height, while a cold, heavy-molecule, high-gravity one stays thin. NASA's NSSDCA planetary fact sheets list Earth's scale height at 8.5 km (surface temperature 288 K, gravity 9.8 m/s^2, surface pressure 1013 mb), Venus at 15.9 km (about 735 K, gravity 8.87 m/s^2, about 92 bar), Mars at 11.1 km (about 210 K average, gravity 3.71 m/s^2, 6.36 mb - about 0.6% of Earth's pressure), and Jupiter at 27 km measured at the standard 1-bar reference level, since Jupiter has no solid surface (about 165 K there, gravity about 24.79 m/s^2). Titan, Saturn's moon, has a surface temperature of about 94 K, gravity of only 1.35 m/s^2, and a surface pressure near 1.5 bar; because its stratosphere is warmed by haze far more than the other four worlds' near-surface layers are, published sources (cross-referenced from Wikipedia's "Atmosphere of Titan" summary of Cassini and Huygens-era atmospheric-structure work) give its scale height as a range of roughly 15 to 50 km depending on altitude, rather than one settled number - this scene uses 20 km, the commonly-cited near-surface estimate.

The five-column atmosphere comparison and the altitude probe are drawn straight on your own device with WebGL - the scale-height figures and decay curves never leave your browser and nothing is uploaded to a server. The 3D engine downloads a single time (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached, so re-dragging the altitude slider or revisiting this explorer later starts instantly.

This is an educational approximation. Real atmospheres do not decay as one perfect exponential all the way up - temperature and composition change with altitude, especially on Titan - so this scene uses a single fixed near-surface scale height per world as a teaching simplification. Every scale height, temperature, gravity, and pressure figure shown is real and independently published by NASA's NSSDCA planetary fact sheets (Earth, Venus, Mars, Jupiter) and, for Titan, cross-referenced published atmospheric-structure sources.
