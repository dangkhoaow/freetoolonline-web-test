Gravitational microlensing is an astronomical technique that detects exoplanets by observing how a foreground star's gravity magnifies and bends light from a distant background star. When the two stars align, the foreground star acts as a lens, creating a magnification spike in the background star's brightness. If an exoplanet orbits the foreground star, it creates an additional brightening spike - revealing the planet's presence.

How it works: A massive star's gravitational field bends spacetime, causing light rays to curve toward the star rather than traveling straight. When a background star's light passes through this curved spacetime, its image becomes magnified and magnified unevenly - an effect discovered by Einstein and now used to detect exoplanets billions of kilometers away.

Key figures (real published data):

  • Magnification formula: A = 0.5 × (u + sqrt(u² + 4)) / u, where u is the alignment angle in Einstein radii
  • Peak magnification: 1.34 at perfect alignment (u = 0)
  • Einstein radius: Typically 0.3 to 3 milliarcseconds for stellar-mass lenses
  • Exoplanet detection sensitivity: Microlensing detects planets at orbital distances of 0.5 to 10 AU from their parent star
  • Event timescale: A typical microlensing event lasts days to weeks as the two stars' relative motion carries them out of alignment

This visualizer shows: A foreground massive star (yellow) bends light rays from a distant background star (white). An exoplanet (blue) orbits the foreground star, and its gravity contributes additional magnification - creating brightening spikes that reveal its presence. The rays gradually curve toward the massive star due to its gravitational pull, demonstrating the lens effect.

Educational approximation: This scene is not a full relativistic simulation. It visualizes the key concept - light bending around a massive object and exoplanet-induced brightening - using simplified procedural geometry. The actual phenomenon involves spacetime curvature and precise ephemeris calculations, but the basic physics is captured here.

Privacy & how it runs: This 3D scene runs entirely in your browser using the vendored three.js WebGL engine. No data is uploaded, no server processing occurs - your computer does all the rendering.
