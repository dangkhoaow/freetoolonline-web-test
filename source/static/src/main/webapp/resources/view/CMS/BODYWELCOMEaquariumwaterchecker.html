Aquarium Water Checker - Compare Before & After Photos

Last reviewed: 2026-09-05

The Aquarium Water Checker compares two photos of the same fish tank taken at different times and measures which one is clearer. It scores each photo's optical clarity from 0 to 100, names the cloudiness pattern it sees, and lists prioritized next steps - entirely in your browser, with neither photo uploaded anywhere.

Why compare two photos instead of judging one

Eyes are poor at judging slow change. You look at the tank every day, so a haze that built up over a week reads as normal, and a bloom that is already fading still looks alarming. Two photos taken days apart make the change visible again: the swipe-to-compare slider lets you scrub between the before and after states, and the measured numbers say whether clarity moved up or down and by how much. That answers the question every fishkeeper actually has after a water change, a filter cleaning, a bacteria dose or a new tank setup: is it getting better or worse?

What the Aquarium Water Checker measures

Each photo is downscaled onto an invisible canvas and read pixel by pixel on your device. Four measurements drive the report. Edge sharpness looks at how crisp the background objects are - a heater, an intake tube, plant stems - because suspended particles soften those edges long before the water looks obviously cloudy. Haze checks how much the darkest areas of the frame have been lifted toward the bright areas: in clear water real shadows stay dark, while scattered light from suspended solids fills them in. Particle speckle measures fine grainy texture in the midtones, which separates mechanical dust from smooth biological haze. Color tint classifies the overall cast - milky, green, amber or neutral. The clarity score combines sharpness, haze and contrast into one 0-100 number per photo, so the delta between the two photos is a single figure you can track week over week.

Reading the cloudiness patterns

The same "cloudy water" complaint has different causes with different fixes, and they look different to a pixel analysis. A uniform milky haze with low speckle is the classic appearance of a heterotrophic bacterial bloom, common in new tanks and after gravel disturbance or overfeeding; the usual response is to feed less and wait rather than to change lots of water. Grainy speckle points to mechanical particles such as unrinsed substrate dust or a stirred-up bottom, which fine filter floss clears in a day or two. A green shift is the free-floating algae direction and responds to a shorter lighting period. A warm amber tint with sharp edges usually means tannins from driftwood or matured water, which is transparent and harmless. The tool phrases each verdict as an optical observation, because that is what a photo can honestly support.

Taking two photos that compare fairly

Shoot both photos from the same spot, at the same angle, under the same lighting - same time of day, same room lights, tank light on in both. Turn off the camera flash, because flash glare rewrites the shadows the haze measurement depends on. Include some fixed background detail such as the filter intake or heater, since those objects are what the sharpness measurement locks onto. The checker measures the exposure difference between your two shots and warns you when they differ too much to score a fair delta; when a photo carries a capture time in its metadata, that is read on your device and used to state how many hours or days apart the two states are.

Stocking estimate and the optional AI passes

Enter your tank volume and fish counts to add a stocking-pressure estimate to the report: a rule-of-thumb waste load per 10 liters with a band from lightly stocked to overstocked, plus a water-change cadence hint. Two optional AI passes can help, and both run on your device. If your browser ships built-in AI, one click rewrites the measured numbers as a short plain-language assessment with nothing downloaded. Separately, an on-device vision model can look at both photos, cross-check the cloudiness verdict, and suggest which fish it thinks it sees; that model is about 147 MB, downloads only after you click and see the size, is cached by the browser for next time, and its species suggestions only prefill the stocking form for you to confirm or correct. When no AI is available, the full pixel-measurement report works exactly the same.

What a photo cannot tell you

No photograph can measure ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, pH, hardness or temperature. Crystal-clear water can still carry an invisible ammonia spike, and a harmless bacterial bloom can look dramatic while the chemistry stays safe. Treat this checker as your trend instrument between tests: it tells you whether the visible situation is improving or worsening and what pattern the cloudiness matches, while a liquid test kit stays the ground truth for water chemistry. If fish are gasping at the surface or resting on the bottom with clamped fins, test the water now regardless of what any photo shows.
