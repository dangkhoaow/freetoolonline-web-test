Six body regions answer to a tap, and four buttons under the model change how the scene moves. The side panel needs no click to start: it carries the published length, mass, era, and diet as soon as the scene loads.

  • The view opens three-quarters around the body at a distance picked for its build, and the zoom and tilt limits keep it from flipping past upright

What is known about Abelisaurus

Abelisaurus is known from a partial skull from the Anacleto Formation of Argentina. Named by Bonaparte and Novas in 1985, it was the first abelisaurid to receive a formal name. Published length and mass estimates span a wide range, so the viewer poses the animal at mid-range values.

How the model is posed, lit, and rendered

A curved spine carries a shaped skull with a hinged, slightly open jaw and serrated teeth; the hind legs stand digitigrade on their toes and the short forelimbs sit tucked, the way abelisaurids are known to have carried them. The silhouette follows how a running theropod is now reconstructed - back roughly level, long tail held clear of the ground to counterbalance the body over the hips, neck curving up to carry the head forward - not the older hunched, tail-dragging pose. Forward-facing eyes, a claw on each toe, and two on the short arm complete the build, and a warm key light high to one side with a cooler fill from behind gives the body modeled shading rather than a flat cutout, above an earth-toned ground surface.

Rendering runs on your device with WebGL and needs no account. On a phone or low-core machine the viewer drops antialiasing to keep the scene moving. With WebGL switched off the page says so in plain words and leaves the fact table above readable; with scripting blocked, a short note explains the 3D view needs JavaScript. The engine is a one-time 0.7 MB cached download shared by every later dinosaur viewer here. If a free-licensed detailed model is published for this species it swaps in without a page reload, scaled and grounded to the same frame; otherwise the procedural model stays.

What the fossil records and what is estimated

Fossils do not preserve color or soft tissue, so the skin tone and pattern are an artistic reconstruction, and each part note draws its own line between preserved bone and informed estimate rather than presenting the whole animal as equally certain.

The skull shape follows the bone while lips and color stay interpretive; the finger count on the short arm is what the fossil hand records; stance and speed read from limb-bone thickness plus preserved trackways; the neck's vertebra count and length come from the fossil while its held posture stays debated; the torso is volume estimated from the rib and hip bones plus their muscle-attachment scars. Some abelisaurids are thought to have used the tail as a weapon or for propulsion, and the short arm is read as strongly muscled even though it is stubby. When the swapped-in file is a real digitised specimen scan, the panel says so and marks the length, mass, and era figures as real published values.

How the abelisaurid siblings differ

Close abelisaurid relatives share the same deep, short skull, yet each carried its own head ornament - paired brow horns on Carnotaurus, a single skull-roof horn on Majungasaurus - so the sibling viewers show noticeably different faces on a similar frame: the Carnotaurus 3D Viewer and the Majungasaurus 3D Viewer.
