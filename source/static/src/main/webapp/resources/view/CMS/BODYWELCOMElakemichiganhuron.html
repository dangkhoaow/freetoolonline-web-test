Lake Michigan-Huron 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-07-27

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

Maps show Lake Michigan and Lake Huron as two separate Great Lakes, split by Michigan's Lower Peninsula. This browser explorer shows why hydrologists treat them as one lake: both basins sit at the exact same water-surface elevation, connected through the Straits of Mackinac at the northern tip of the peninsula. Toggle between separate lake colors and a single unified color to see the connection, or highlight the shallow strait itself.

Lake Michigan reaches about 281 m at its deepest point and covers about 57,750 sq km. Lake Huron is slightly larger by surface area at about 59,590 sq km but shallower, reaching about 229 m at its deepest point. Combined, the two basins cover about 117,340 sq km of surface area - larger than Lake Superior alone, making Michigan-Huron the largest connected body of fresh water on Earth by surface area, even though Superior remains deeper and holds more total volume. The connecting Straits of Mackinac are only about 5.6 km wide at the narrowest point and about 50 m deep - a shallow doorway compared to either basin.

Because both named basins and the shallow Straits of Mackinac connector sit in a single scene, the whole model rotates and redraws on your own device using the site's vendored three.js build - nothing about the visit goes to a server. That one-time download (about 0.7 MB) stays cached in the browser, so coming back to compare the strait against either lake later loads instantly.

This is an educational approximation - the basin profile, connecting strait, and vertical scale are schematic teaching cues, not survey data. The Straits view is different: it streams real measured elevation (Mapzen/AWS Open Data terrain tiles, derived from NASA/USGS DEMs) and real satellite imagery (Copernicus Sentinel-2) of the Straits of Mackinac connector and Mackinac Island, so that part of the scene is genuine surveyed terrain, not a teaching approximation - Terrarium elevation data records each lake's surface, not its underwater depth, which is why each lake's own maximum-depth figure stays in the schematic two-basin view above.
