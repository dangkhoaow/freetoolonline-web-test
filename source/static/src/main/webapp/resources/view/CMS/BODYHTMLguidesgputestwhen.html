When GPU Test Fits - and When It Does Not

Last reviewed: 2026-07-12

GPU Test answers one question fast: what can this browser's graphics stack actually do, and how does it hold up under load. It needs no install and nothing you type or click ever leaves the browser. Below are the situations it serves well, and the ones where a different tool is the better pick.


A quick capability check before anything else

Open the page and a report renders instantly, before the 3D engine even loads: WebGL 1 and 2 availability, the renderer and vendor strings your browser reports (or a note that they are masked for privacy), max texture size, max render buffer, max viewport, MSAA sample count, and whether the browser exposes WebGPU. That is enough to confirm whether this machine supports WebGL 2 or whether this browser exposes WebGPU, without running anything further.


Tracking your own device over time

Run the benchmark and your last 20 runs, including date, score, top tier held, FPS, resolution, and renderer string, stay in this browser's local storage, so you can watch whether a driver update, a browser update, or a new laptop moved the number. Nothing is uploaded, so the comparison only makes sense against your own past runs on the same device and browser.


Shared, work, or locked-down computers

The whole test is client-side: the capability report comes from raw browser calls, and the benchmark renders on your own device via a vendored copy of three.js, about 0.7 MB, downloaded once and cached. There is nothing to install and nothing to upload, which makes it a comfortable pick on a work laptop or a shared family computer.


Where it does not fit

GPU Test is honest about its limits: it is an indicative browser benchmark, not a lab-grade suite. It does not measure video memory, compute or ML throughput, thermals, or power draw, only instanced-geometry and presentation throughput through the browser's graphics stack. Device heat, battery-saver mode, and other open tabs all shift the score, so treat runs on different devices as not directly comparable, and do not expect a hardware identification: some browsers mask the renderer string for privacy, and the page says so rather than guessing.
