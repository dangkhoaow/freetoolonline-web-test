Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Mount Fuji so perfectly symmetrical?

Mount Fuji is a stratovolcano - a steep-sided composite volcano built up by many layers of hardened lava, pumice, and volcanic ash. Its iconic symmetrical cone shape reflects the gravity-driven collapse of each eruption's ejecta around the central vent. The mountain's last eruption (1707-1708, Hoei eruption) added material that reinforced this perfect profile. Geological surveys confirm the cone angle is remarkably regular - approximately 32-35 degrees from base to peak on all sides.

When does Mount Fuji have its snow cap?

The snow cap typically forms around October as autumn cooling begins and persists through winter and spring. Most snow melts by mid-July. The exact timing varies with annual weather - early/late springs can shift the melt window by 2-3 weeks. Summer climbing season (July-September) is mostly snow-free above the mid-slopes, though small patches may persist in shaded cirques year-round.

What is the forest zone on Mount Fuji?

The forested slopes (roughly 0-2,400 m elevation) transition through four vegetation zones. Base foothills have broadleaf deciduous trees (oak, maple). Mid-slopes (600-1,500 m) feature Japanese larch and Japanese hemlock. Upper slopes (1,500-2,400 m) transition to subalpine conifers (dwarf bamboo understorey). Above 2,400 m, vegetation becomes sparse - dwarf pine and cushion plants cling to volcanic rock. Above 3,200 m, the mountain is essentially barren rock and ice - the alpine zone.

Is Mount Fuji still an active volcano?

Mount Fuji is classified as an active volcano but has not erupted since 1707-1708. The last eruption (Hoei eruption) was relatively small by historical standards - it lasted about 49 days and produced ash fall across much of Honshu. Seismic and gas-emission monitoring continues. The volcano is considered dormant, not extinct, and Japanese authorities maintain monitoring networks and emergency protocols.
