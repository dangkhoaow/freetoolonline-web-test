Mercury rotates exactly 3 times for every 2 orbits around the Sun - a 3:2 spin-orbit resonance that produces a 176-Earth-day solar day (sunrise to sunrise) despite a sidereal day of only 59 Earth days. The 3:2 Resonance Explained

Mercury's resonance is not a coincidence - it is a tidal-locking effect refined over billions of years. Mercury likely rotated faster early in the solar system's history; as it orbited, the Sun's gravity gradually dissipated rotational energy and slowed its spin. It did not settle into perfect 1:1 synchronization as our Moon did, because Mercury's orbital eccentricity is high enough (0.206) to make the 3:2 state the stable one instead: 3 days (rotations) equals 2 years (orbits). That state minimizes the long-term average solar torque and is a stable equilibrium, meaning small perturbations decay back to it rather than growing.

The mathematics: Mercury's sidereal period is 87.969 Earth days. Its rotation period is 58.646 Earth days. The ratio is 87.969 / 58.646 ≈ 1.5 = 3:2. One consequence of stretching the solar day this far is that some regions of Mercury stay in daylight for 88 Earth days at a stretch, while others sit in prolonged darkness.

Why Mercury (Not All Planets)

Not every planet experiences tidal locking or resonance as dramatically as Mercury. Venus is in an unusual 5:2 resonance with Earth (unrelated to Venus-Sun tidal effects). Earth's Moon is in 1:1 resonance with Earth because its orbit is close and nearly circular. Mercury's situation is unique because it is close to the Sun, has significant orbital eccentricity, and is small enough that tidal forces have had time to reshape its rotation over billions of years.

Privacy & How It Works

This 3D scene runs entirely in your browser. No data is sent to external servers. The scene is rendered using WebGL and the vendored three.js library. Time-scrub state is saved to your browser's local storage, so if you reload the page, your position in the Mercurian year is preserved.

The resonance itself wasn't confirmed until 1965, when radar observations by Gordon Pettengill and Rolf Dyce disproved the earlier assumption that Mercury was 1:1 tidally locked like the Moon. One visible result: two longitudes on Mercury act as "hot poles" that face the Sun directly only at alternating perihelion passes rather than every one, so each hot pole peaks in temperature every other Mercurian year rather than every year. The Mercury Spin-Orbit Resonance 3D Explorer scrubs through a full 176-day solar day and marks both hot poles as they swap which one faces the Sun.
