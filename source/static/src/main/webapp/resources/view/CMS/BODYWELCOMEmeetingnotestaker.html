Meeting Notes Taker

Last reviewed: 2026-08-29

Press record, hold your meeting, press stop: the recording is transcribed with a speech model that runs inside this browser tab, and the transcript is turned into draft minutes you can edit, copy or download.

Typical use: a standup, a client call or an interview you want written up without handing the audio to a note-taking service.

What the minutes contain

The notes are assembled from the transcript in six sections. Summary picks the sentences that carry the most repeated content words, in the order they were said. Decisions collects lines with decision language ("we agreed", "approved", "we are going with"). Action items collects lines with commitment language ("I will send", "let us schedule", "needs to") and, when the sentence names one, the deadline it mentions. Risks and blockers, open questions and figures mentioned follow the same idea for problems, questions and any money, percentage or count that came up. Every line keeps its timecode, so you can jump back to the moment in the recording and check it.

This is deliberately a text-analysis pass, not a model writing prose: it can only surface sentences that were actually spoken, so it cannot invent a decision nobody made. On a computer with WebGPU there is an optional button that asks a small language model, also running in this tab, to rewrite the same notes as prose - it appears next to the extracted version rather than replacing it, because a small model can still drift.

Three ways to get the audio in

Choose one at the top of the tool. Microphone only is the simplest and works in every browser that can record. Microphone plus the audio of a tab or window is the option for online calls: Chrome and Edge on a computer let you share a tab and tick "share tab audio", and the tool mixes that with your microphone so both sides of the conversation are transcribed. Upload a recording takes an audio or video file you already have - a Zoom recording, a voice memo, an MP3 - reads it in the tab and transcribes it the same way. Recording other people is your responsibility; ask for consent before you start.

What the first run costs

The speech model is OpenAI Whisper base, about 78 MB in its quantised ONNX form. The tool shows the real download percentage, then the browser keeps the files, so later meetings start with no wait and transcription still works offline. Speed depends on the machine: with WebGPU a recording transcribes several times faster than real time, while on the CPU it runs at roughly the length of the audio it is given - and how much audio that is depends on the silence pass described below. The status line says which mode is in use, so the number is never a surprise. Long meetings are processed in 30 second chunks, so memory stays flat rather than growing with the recording.

Why the wait is shorter than the meeting

A speech model costs time in proportion to the audio it is handed, and a meeting is mostly pauses. So the recording is examined first by a 0.1 MB WebAssembly voice detector, which scores every ten milliseconds of sound; only the spans where somebody is speaking go to Whisper. Three details keep that safe rather than merely fast: three tenths of a second are kept either side of each span so words are not clipped, gaps shorter than six tenths of a second are never cut because that is a breath, and the whole pass is abandoned when it would save less than a twentieth of the recording.

Timings are the part that matters for trust. The model only sees the shortened audio, so the timestamps it returns belong to that version - useless for finding a moment in the real meeting. Each kept span therefore remembers where it came from, and every timecode is converted back before you see it. Measured on a test clip, four short contributions spread over 28 seconds came to 7.8 seconds of speech, with the timecodes landing on the original timeline with no measurable error.

It makes transcription quicker, never more accurate, and a detector deciding what counts as a voice can be wrong about a whisper or someone far from the microphone. The padding makes that unlikely rather than impossible, which is why the pass is a tick box: untick it and every second goes to the model, exactly as the page behaved before.

Where this sits next to the other tools

The speech to text tool is for live dictation: it types what you say into a box as you speak, using the recognition service built into your browser. This page is for the opposite shape of work - a whole meeting, captured first and processed afterwards, entirely on your device, ending in a document rather than a text box. If you need the reverse direction, text to speech reads written text aloud with neural voices.

Privacy, licences and credit

No audio, transcript or note is uploaded: the model files come down to your browser and the processing happens locally, which is also why the tool keeps working with the network disconnected. The speech model is Apache-2.0 licensed and served from our own asset server at a pinned version, checksummed when it is published. The voice detector is rnnoise-wasm (Apache-2.0), a build of Xiph RNNoise (BSD-3-Clause), loaded at an exact version and checked against a known digest every time before it runs. The idea comes from meetily, an MIT-licensed desktop meeting assistant; that project is a Rust and Tauri application with no browser build, so this page is an independent implementation of the same job for the browser rather than a port of its code.
