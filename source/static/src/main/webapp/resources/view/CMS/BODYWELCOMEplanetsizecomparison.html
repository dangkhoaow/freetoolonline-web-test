Everything renders on your device with WebGL through the vendored three.js engine, which loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached - no account, no upload, and no scene data sent to a server.

The scene is an educational size comparison tuned to teach planetary scale - it does not show heliocentric distances, orbital motion, or mass. The spacing between worlds is widened for readability only and carries no distance meaning.

Depth: how this differs from the Solar System 3D Explorer

The two scenes keep different geometry honest, because they answer different questions. Fitting orbiting planets onto one screen forces the Solar System 3D Explorer to compress both the sphere sizes and the gaps between orbits - Neptune would otherwise sit about thirty times farther out than Earth and vanish off-frame. This page makes the opposite trade: it never fakes a radius, so Jupiter's 69,911 km sphere stays roughly eleven times wider than Earth's 6,371 km and the Sun at 696,340 km dwarfs every world, and the spin rotates the lineup in place rather than tracing orbits. Reach for the orbit-first view when you want layout and motion; stay here when the point is true proportional size - Uranus at 25,362 km sitting only just larger than Neptune at 24,622 km is a comparison an orbit-first scene cannot show without distorting it.
