Loch Ness 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-08-28

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer shows Loch Ness as a cross-section of a straight fault-line trough: steep granite hillsides on both banks, a long near-uniform underwater bowl, and two toggleable markers - one showing how far the true floor lies below the dark surface, and one showing the geological fault line the loch sits in continuing well past both ends of the loch itself.

Loch Ness occupies part of the Great Glen Fault, a geological fault line that slices diagonally across the Scottish Highlands. Unlike a winding fjord or a round volcanic caldera, the loch is remarkably straight and narrow for its length - about 37 km (23 miles) long but only about 1.6 km (1 mile) wide on average. The water is heavily stained by peat washed in from the surrounding hills, which limits visibility to just a few meters even though the true floor drops to about 230 m (755 ft) at its deepest sounding. Between the depth and the length, Loch Ness alone holds more fresh water than all the lakes of England and Wales combined.

  • Toggle the max-depth marker to see the 230 m drop to the loch floor
  • Toggle the Great Glen Fault line to see it run past both ends of the loch
  • Read length, depth, width, and volume figures in the panel
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom

The whole Loch Ness trough - the granite hillsides, the underwater bowl, the depth marker, and the fault-line marker - is built and drawn on your own device with WebGL through the site-vendored three.js engine. Orbit the scene to look along the length of the loch, and toggle the markers to compare the shallow visibility depth against the true depth of the trough. Nothing about your visit is uploaded to a server, and the engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached.

This is an educational approximation - the trough profile, basin depths, and vertical scale are schematic teaching cues, not survey bathymetry.
