Congo River 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-08-28

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer traces the Congo River as one incised channel through equatorial rainforest - a winding groove cut through the terrain, never fanning into distributaries - with a yellow marker showing how far the deepest sounding, over 220 m, sits below the surface.

The Congo is the only major river that crosses the equator twice, so its two river-basin halves have offsetting rainy seasons and the combined flow stays unusually steady year-round - a rare trait among the world's great rivers. Near its mouth the river drops through the Livingstone Falls, roughly 350 km of rapids and cataracts losing about 270 m of elevation before reaching the Atlantic. Those falls are why no direct shipping route has ever connected the deep interior to the ocean, and why a rail line was built to bypass them instead.

  • Toggle the deepest-point marker to see the 220 m+ sounding against the surface
  • Toggle the rapids markers to see the Livingstone Falls stretch near the mouth
  • Read length, depth, discharge, and basin-area figures in the panel
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom
  • Runs fully in the browser with the vendored three.js engine - no account, no upload

For a river that fans outward instead of cutting a single deep channel, open the Okavango Delta 3D Explorer, or compare against the flood-season plume of the Amazon River 3D Explorer.

The whole Congo River scene - the winding channel groove, the depth-color banding, the canopy decor, and the rapids markers near the mouth - is built and drawn on your own device with WebGL through the site-vendored three.js engine. Orbit the scene to follow the river's two big equatorial bends, and zoom toward the yellow marker to see how deep 220 m really reaches below the rainforest floor. Nothing about your visit is uploaded to a server, and the engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached.

This is an educational approximation - the channel path, bend geometry, depth banding, and rapids placement are schematic teaching cues, not survey data.
