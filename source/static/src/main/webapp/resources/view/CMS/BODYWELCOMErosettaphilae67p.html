Rosetta + Philae 67P 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-09-09

Rosetta is the mission that put the first lander on a comet. It caught up with 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko on August 6, 2014, released Philae onto the nucleus on November 12, 2014, rode the comet through its closest approach to the Sun on August 13, 2015, and ended on September 30, 2016 with a controlled descent onto the nucleus itself.

Controls, and the one detail the timeline compresses

Drag to orbit the view, scroll or pinch to zoom, and press Play or Pause to animate the timeline. Drag the progress slider to jump straight to any of the seven real mission milestones, or click the comet, Rosetta, or the Philae lander to refresh the facts panel. The facts panel carries the published date and figures for whichever milestone you land on, each one sourced in the table below.

One detail the timeline compresses: after the anchoring harpoons failed to fire at Agilkia, Philae bounced for roughly two hours across about four surface contacts before coming to rest at Abydos. That is why the first landing on a comet did not go as planned, and why the mission is also a story about recovering a working lander anyway.

What the Rosetta and Philae scene renders

  • Procedural two-lobed comet nucleus built from two overlapping bumped spheres, matching 67P's real "contact binary" shape - the shape comets take when two smaller bodies stick together
  • Philae visibly separates from Rosetta, descends, bounces between the Agilkia and Abydos landing sites, and comes to rest
  • A perihelion note and particle jets appear near the comet's closest approach to the Sun, showing the real increase in outgassing activity

How the Rosetta and Philae scene is built, and its limits

Everything renders on your device with WebGL through the vendored three.js engine, which loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is then cached. No account, no upload, and no scene data is sent to a server. The scene is an educational approximation of the mission arc, not a precomputed ephemeris of the real trajectory and not a contact-dynamics solver of Philae's bounce: the comet's two-lobed shape, orbit path, descent arc, and landing-site positions are simplified for legibility, and the table above holds the real published dates, distances, and figures.
