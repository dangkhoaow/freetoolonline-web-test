Io Plasma Torus 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-09-06

This browser explorer shows the doughnut-shaped ring of ionized gas that circles Jupiter along Io's orbit, isolates its three real radial layers, and reveals a documented bright sector that laps Io's own orbit as it rotates with Jupiter's magnetic field.

Io's volcanoes supply the torus

Io, the innermost of Jupiter's four large Galilean moons, is the most volcanically active body known in the solar system. Its volcanoes vent about 1 tonne of neutral gas - mostly sulfur and oxygen compounds - into space every second. Jupiter's fast-rotating magnetic field ionizes that gas and drags it around the planet, building a persistent doughnut-shaped plasma cloud centered on Io's orbit at 5.9 Jupiter radii (RJ).

Three radial layers make up the structure

The torus is not one uniform ring. Three radial regions make it up, first mapped by Voyager 1 and refined by Cassini and Juno: a cold inner disk under about 5.5 RJ, a dense warm ribbon from about 5.5 to 7.6 RJ that contains Io's own orbit, and an extended hot outer torus beyond about 7.6 RJ. Electron temperature climbs steeply outward across the three zones while the warm ribbon carries the density peak; the table below gives each layer's measured values and source.

A bright sector laps Io four times per orbit

The torus is not evenly bright all the way around. Cassini found a denser, brighter sector that rotates with Jupiter's magnetic field every 10.07 hours, about 1.5 percent longer than Jupiter's own System III rotation period. Io orbits far slower, once every 42.5 hours, so that bright sector sweeps past Io roughly four times during each of Io's orbits. Cassini's UVIS instrument also measured the whole torus radiating about 1.7 terawatts of extreme-ultraviolet light, varying by up to 25 percent as the torus's density and composition shift over time.

What you can change in this scene

  • A layer button cycles through the cold disk, warm ribbon, and extended hot torus, isolating each one on screen
  • A bulge button shows or hides the real co-rotating bright sector as it laps Io's slower orbit
  • A facts panel updates with the published density, temperature, and radiated-power figures for the selected layer
  • Io and the torus keep their own real relative rotation rates, so the four-to-one lapping ratio you see is genuine, not staged
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom
  • Renders on your device with WebGL on the vendored three.js engine - no account, no upload; the engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is cached

Where this scene simplifies

The particle counts, colors, and the bulge's exact shape are an illustrative teaching device, not a calibrated emission-intensity map or a plasma-transport simulation. Everything in the table above is a real published value.
