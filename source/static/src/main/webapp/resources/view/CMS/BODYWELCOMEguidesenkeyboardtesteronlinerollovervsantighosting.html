Depth: NKRO = every simultaneous key registers

N-key rollover and anti-ghosting sound like the same feature, but a keyboard can pass one test and fail the other. Rollover is a count: 6-key rollover registers six held keys plus modifiers and silently drops the seventh, while true NKRO registers every key no matter how many you hold at once. Anti-ghosting is a wiring fix that stops phantom keys appearing when three keys share one matrix line - the classic WASD-plus-Q chord is where a cheap 2KRO board either loses the Q or invents a fourth keypress. To separate the two in the on-screen keyboard test, hold WASD and count the highlighted keys first (that measures rollover), then add Q and watch for any key that lights up on its own (that exposes ghosting). Budget boards often advertise anti-ghosting on a fixed set of gaming keys - roughly the WASD keys and the arrows - while leaving the rest of the matrix at 2KRO, so a chord across the number row can still fail even when the gaming keys look perfect.

What the two results mean is where the terms finally separate. A capped rollover shows up as a hard ceiling: a 6-key-rollover board displays at most six letters plus modifiers and simply ignores the seventh, so a long chord in a game silently loses inputs even though nothing looks broken. Ghosting is the opposite failure - a key you never pressed lights up, because three switches sitting at three corners of a rectangle in the switch matrix trick a cheap board into reporting the empty fourth corner. That is why one keyboard can pass one test and fail the other: an office board often shows no phantom keys during light typing yet stalls at two or three simultaneous presses, while a full-NKRO board with per-key anti-ghosting clears both because every key has its own reliable path back to the controller. When you run the keyboard test, read a missing key as a rollover limit and a self-lit key as a ghost - they point at two different hardware causes, not one.

There is a catch in how you press the keys, because the two faults show up at different chord sizes. Ghosting appears with as few as three keys - hold three corners of a rectangle on the layout, such as E, R and D, and watch whether F lights up on its own. The rollover ceiling, though, stays hidden until you exceed the count: a 6-key-rollover board happily shows any three, four, five or six keys and only drops the seventh, so a five-key chord like WASD plus Q proves nothing about rollover and tests only ghosting. To measure the ceiling instead, hold seven or more letter keys at once and count the highlighted boxes; if the display stops at six no matter how many more you add, that board is 6KRO, while a full-NKRO board keeps lighting every key you hold. Modifier keys such as Shift and Ctrl usually sit on their own lines and register on top of that six, so count only letter and number keys, or a genuine six-key cap can look like seven.

There is also a reason so many keyboards stop at exactly six keys, and it lives in the USB connection rather than the switches. The standard USB HID boot protocol - the mode a keyboard uses before any driver loads - reports at most six regular keys plus eight modifiers (the left and right Shift, Ctrl, Alt and Windows keys) in a single packet, so a board wired for full NKRO still shows only 6KRO until the operating system switches it into a richer report mode. That is why a keyboard advertised as NKRO can cap at six keys inside the BIOS setup screen or the Windows login field, then register every key once the desktop loads and the full descriptor takes over. When you count highlighted keys in the on-screen test, do it from inside the browser on a fully booted desktop, because running the same chord in a boot menu measures the USB fallback rather than the keyboard's real ceiling. A PS/2 connector sidesteps the limit entirely because it sends each key as its own interrupt, which is why a few enthusiast boards still ship with a PS/2 plug for guaranteed NKRO.

There is a physical reason the two features usually travel together, and it comes down to one tiny component sitting behind each key. Full NKRO almost always needs a diode wired behind every switch - a one-way gate that blocks the stray current backflow that would otherwise light a fourth, never-pressed key - so the same per-key diode that guarantees rollover is also what delivers genuine anti-ghosting. Membrane boards skip those diodes to shave cost, which is why a cheap office keyboard types fine yet ghosts the instant you cross three keys, while a mechanical board with per-key diodes clears both tests at once. That is also why a spec sheet is never proof: a listing that reads "26-key rollover" or "anti-ghosting on 19 gaming keys" is describing a partial matrix, not full NKRO, and only the on-screen test shows which keys on your own unit actually hold. Because diode coverage on a budget board often stops at the gaming keys, press the chords you actually use across the number row and the right hand, where an unprotected key is most likely to drop or double.

Once you can tell the two faults apart, the practical question is which one your own use actually depends on, and that splits by how many keys you really hold at once. Someone typing prose or code rarely exceeds two or three keys together, so a modest 6-key rollover is plenty; what matters for them is clean handling of the modifier chords a shortcut relies on, such as Ctrl plus Shift plus an arrow or Ctrl plus Alt plus a letter, where one dropped or invented key fires the wrong command. A gamer blows past six in a single motion - holding W, A and Shift to strafe-sprint while tapping Space to jump and a number key to swap weapons is already five or six live keys - so a higher rollover count is the feature that keeps the sixth input from vanishing mid-fight. To decide for your own case rather than trusting the box, reproduce your busiest real chord in the on-screen test - the exact keys from your heaviest shortcut or game moment - and hold it for a second while you watch whether every key lights up and nothing else on the layout moves. The two limits are fixed by different things when you shop: a low rollover count is a number you can buy past with a higher-rated board, while a matrix that garbles one particular region of keys is set by the wiring under those switches and reads the same on every reboot.

Underneath both tests sits one piece of hardware - a grid of wires the controller scans row by row, energizing a single row at a time and reading back which columns answer, and every key is simply one row-and-column junction on that grid. Rollover falls out of how many of those junctions the controller will report in a single pass, so it is one fixed number for the whole board, which is exactly why a spec sheet can quote 6KRO or full NKRO as a single figure. Ghosting is a geometry problem tied to the same grid: because each key is a junction, a few keys sharing one row and one column can let the scan misread an untouched junction as pressed, and precisely which keys collide is fixed by how your particular board maps its layout onto those rows and columns. That is why the chord that ghosts is never universal - one board fails on WASD plus Q while another only trips on a trio buried in the number row - and it is the concrete reason NKRO and anti-ghosting stay separate features rather than two names for one thing. The takeaway for the on-screen keyboard test is that you cannot borrow someone else's ghosting chord as proof about your own unit; press the exact junctions your hand reaches during your heaviest typing or gaming, and watch for any key that lights with no finger on it.
