Adaptive refresh rate (ProMotion, LTPO, variable refresh)

Many modern screens do not run at one fixed speed. Recent iPhone Pro and iPad Pro models and 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro displays use ProMotion panels that shift between low rates and 120 Hz depending on what is on screen, and gaming monitors with variable refresh can move across a wide range such as 48 to 165 Hz.

What the Adaptive refresh tracking panel records

  • The minimum and maximum rate seen during the session.
  • Each stable rate the browser held for a moment - for example both ~60 Hz and ~120 Hz.
  • A small chart showing how much time was spent near each common rate.
  • A "rate switching observed" verdict when two clearly different stable rates appear - the behavior signature of an adaptive display.
  • A small moving dot that animates during measurement, so panels that lower their rate on still content have a reason to speed up.

If the reading looks lower than expected

The browser only exposes frame timing, so this test observes how the display behaves right now; it cannot read the panel's full supported range from the hardware. Some devices also cap web animation below the panel maximum.

  • An iPhone in Low Power Mode, or Safari before its full-rate setting is enabled, may report ~60 Hz even on a 120 Hz screen.
  • If the number looks low, plug in, disable power saving, and try again.

How the frame readout decides you have an adaptive panel

After you press Start measuring, each animation frame feeds a 20-frame rolling window that turns the gap between frames into an instantaneous rate, so the large Hz number you watch is a smoothed estimate rather than a single raw reading. A run of frames that stays within 8% of one speed for at least 30 frames is logged as a distinct stable rate, which is why a ProMotion or LTPO screen often lists two plateaus - roughly 60 Hz while the page sits still and roughly 120 Hz once motion starts - instead of one flat number. The panel only prints "rate switching observed" when two of those plateaus differ by 15% or more, a deliberately high bar that keeps ordinary timing jitter from being mistaken for a variable-refresh display. Every rolling-window rate is also dropped into fixed buckets from 24 through 240 Hz (24/30/48/60/75/90/120/144/165/240) and drawn as percentage bars, so a session that spent most of its time near 60 and a short burst near 120 reads as two bars rather than a single average that hides the switch. One concrete edge case worth knowing: on an iPhone the panel watches whether the session maximum stays at or below about 65 Hz, and if it does it explains that Safari caps web animation near 60 Hz on a 120 Hz ProMotion panel - Low Power Mode applies the same ceiling - so a "low" result there reflects the browser's frame cap, not the hardware. Pressing Stop freezes the last estimate for reading and Reset clears the samples so the plateau and bucket history start clean on your next run.
