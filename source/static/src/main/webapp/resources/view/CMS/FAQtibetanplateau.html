Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the Tibetan Plateau so high?

The Tibetan Plateau was formed by the collision of the Indian and Asian tectonic plates starting about 50 million years ago. This collision caused the crust to thicken and buckle upward, creating one of the world's largest high-altitude plateaus. The collision is still ongoing, which is why the region experiences frequent earthquakes and the plateau continues to rise slowly.

Do people live on the Tibetan Plateau?

Yes, about 2-3 million people live on the plateau, mostly Tibetan people along with Han Chinese minorities in urban areas. Traditional communities include nomadic herders who keep yaks, sheep, and dzos (yak-cattle hybrids), and settled farmers in river valleys. The extreme altitude, cold climate, and thin air make settlement challenging - most people live below 4,000 m, with sparse populations in the interior above that elevation.

Why do five major rivers originate on the Tibetan Plateau?

The plateau's high elevation and location in Central Asia make it the natural source region for several of Asia's most important river systems. Rainfall and snowmelt from the plateau's mountains collect into river valleys, which then flow outward toward the Pacific Ocean and Indian Ocean basins. These five rivers (Yangtze, Yellow, Mekong, Salween, and Brahmaputra) together supply water to nearly 2 billion people across Asia.

What is the climate like on the Tibetan Plateau?

The plateau has a semi-arid, high-altitude climate with very cold winters and cool summers. The interior experiences annual temperatures between -5 and 0 degrees Celsius, with severe cold in winter. Rainfall is sparse - less than 500 mm per year in most areas - because the plateau's elevation blocks moisture-laden air masses from reaching the interior. Summer brings brief monsoon rains, but most of the plateau remains dry.

How high is the Tibetan Plateau compared to other places?

The Tibetan Plateau's average elevation of 4,000 m is higher than the highest peaks in the contiguous United States (Mount Whitney at 4.4 km), and rivals the average elevation of the entire Rocky Mountain chain. Standing at the plateau's center puts you nearly as high as the summit of Mount Denali (6.2 km) in Alaska. This extreme elevation makes the plateau one of the most elevated inhabited regions on Earth - human adaptation to the thin air and cold is remarkable.

What does the elevation toggle do in this explorer?

The "Elevation Bands" toggle switches between two visualization modes. When toggled ON, the terrain is colored by elevation - brown for lower areas, progressing through beige and grey to white for the highest peaks. This color gradient helps you see the plateau's internal relief and elevation differences. When toggled OFF, the entire plateau is shown in a single warm plateau tone (beige/tan), allowing you to see the terrain's shape without color coding.

What do the river headwaters represent?

The "River Headwaters" toggle shows the origins of the five major Asian rivers that begin on the Tibetan Plateau: the Yangtze (China's longest river), the Yellow River (China's second-longest), the Mekong (flowing through Southeast Asia), the Salween (flowing to Myanmar and Thailand), and the Brahmaputra (flowing to India and Bangladesh). These ribbon-like paths represent the approximate courses of these rivers as they flow outward from the plateau toward their respective ocean basins, together supplying water to nearly a quarter of the world's population.
