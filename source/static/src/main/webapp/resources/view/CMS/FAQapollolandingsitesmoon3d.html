Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate are the landing site coordinates?

The coordinates come from NASA's LRO-based lunar site tables in the Mean Earth/Polar Axis reference frame, refined using imagery from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. They are accurate to within a few meters - far more precise than the estimates available at the time of each mission.

Why did NASA choose different sites for each mission?

Early missions targeted flat, low-risk mare (lava plain) terrain to prove the landing technique safely. Once that was proven, later missions - Apollo 15, 16, and 17 - targeted highland and mountain-edge sites that offered more scientifically valuable rock samples, at the cost of a harder landing approach.

Is this a physics simulation of the actual landings?

No. This is an educational approximation that plots the real, published landing coordinates on a smooth procedural Moon model. It does not simulate the descent trajectory, engine burns, or terrain the astronauts actually saw - the Moon surface here is a simple sphere, not a rendered elevation map.

Which Apollo mission traveled farthest from the others?

Apollo 12's site in Oceanus Procellarum sits roughly on the opposite side of the visible near side from Apollo 17's Taurus-Littrow site, more than 3,000 kilometers apart in longitude. Use the dropdown to jump between missions and see the spread for yourself.

Were there other planned landing sites that never happened?

Yes. Apollo 18, 19, and 20 were cancelled in 1970 due to budget cuts, along with their planned sites. Apollo 13's planned landing at Fra Mauro was aborted after an oxygen tank failure en route; Apollo 14 later landed at that same site successfully in 1971.

Why is the Moon model smooth instead of showing craters?

The scene favors a fast-loading procedural sphere so the page renders instantly in any browser. The value of this page is the accurate site locations and mission facts, not a textured terrain - see the FAQ note in the facts panel for the same disclosure.
