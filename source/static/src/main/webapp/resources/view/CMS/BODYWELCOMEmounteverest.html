Mount Everest 3D Explorer

Last reviewed: 2026-07-27

Reviewed by FreetoolOnline editorial team

This browser explorer shows Mount Everest as a schematic Himalayan massif: the 8,848.86 m summit beside its neighbours Lhotse and Nuptse, snow above the schematic snowline, and a translucent red band marking the death zone above 8,000 m.

Everest - Sagarmatha in Nepali, Chomolungma in Tibetan - stands on the Nepal / Tibet (China) border in the Mahalangur Himal range. Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay made the first confirmed ascent on 1953-05-29. Above about 8,000 m the air pressure falls to roughly a third of sea level, which is why climbers call it the death zone, and the mountain still rises a few millimetres each year as the Indian plate pushes under Asia.

  • Toggle the death-zone band above 8,000 m
  • Toggle the snow caps above the schematic snowline
  • Read summit, first-ascent, and oxygen figures in the panel
  • Compare Everest with Lhotse (8,516 m) and Nuptse (7,861 m) beside it
  • Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom
  • Runs fully in the browser with the vendored three.js engine - no account, no upload

For the opposite extreme - the lowest land on Earth - open the Dead Sea 3D Explorer, or walk the rock layers of the Grand Canyon 3D Explorer.

Orbiting the Everest massif, toggling the death-zone band, and comparing the summit against Lhotse and Nuptse all happen on your own device: the schematic terrain is drawn with WebGL and the vendored three.js engine loads once (about 0.7 MB) and is cached, so returning to spin the peak or re-read the summit figures needs no re-download and no upload.

After the schematic relief loads, this explorer fetches real elevation data - Terrarium terrain tiles from Mapzen and AWS Open Data, derived from NASA/USGS digital elevation models - plus true-color Sentinel-2 satellite imagery of the Khumbu massif, and swaps them in over the same footprint so the ridgelines and summit shape you see are measured terrain, not a procedural sketch.

This is an educational approximation - ridge shapes, the valley floor, and the snowline are schematic teaching cues, not survey terrain.
