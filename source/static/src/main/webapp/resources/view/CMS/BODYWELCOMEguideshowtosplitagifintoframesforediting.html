More depth: what the frame count and the PNG-versus-JPG choice actually decide

Splitting runs one frame at a time rather than as a single archive, so each still lands in your downloads folder under its own sequence name - frame-001.png, frame-002.png, and so on - which keeps the playback order intact when you later re-assemble the loop. A GIF that runs three seconds at fifteen frames per second decodes into forty-five separate images, and the extractor reports that count plus the per-frame delay in milliseconds before you commit to a download, so an unexpected total (ninety frames instead of forty-five) is a reliable sign the source was really thirty fps. The PNG-versus-JPG pick is the genuine fork: PNG keeps a lossless copy with transparency preserved, which matters when you retouch one frame and drop it back in, while JPG throws away the alpha channel and re-compresses, trading fidelity for a file that is often three to five times smaller. Because the decode happens inside the browser, a two-hundred-frame animation never leaves your machine and the wait scales with your own processor rather than an upload queue. When the edits are done, feeding the same numbered sequence and its original delay back through GIF Maker rebuilds the animation at the timing you started with.
